\begin{abstract}
On 6 October 2026 OpenAI published an initial 722 AI-written mathematical manuscripts, organized into 372 problem families, in the public repository \href{https://github.com/openai/math}{\texttt{github.com/openai/math}}~\cite{src:repo}. The release includes claimed solutions to many prominent open problems. We approach this collection as a treasure mine of mathematical ideas. Our aim is to extract new lemmas, constructions, inequalities and proof mechanisms introduced in the source arguments, and to distinguish them from established tools and new combinations of those tools. We call these \emph{mathematical instruments}. Extraction comes first; their validity, novelty and possible reuse then become subjects for further study. The resulting descriptions also help readers understand the selected papers without having to reconstruct every argument at the outset.
We examine 38 families in two groups of 17 and 21, spanning number theory, geometry, analysis, combinatorics, complexity, algebra, operator algebras, topology and mathematical physics. Each entry records the claim, extracted instruments, antecedents and caveats. Eleven cross-cutting patterns connect the entries, and a scoreboard records formalization status and dependencies. Novelty assessments are provisional and sometimes mixed. Selected source comparisons correct mathematical statements, hypotheses and counts; they do not independently establish priority or certify the underlying proofs. No Lean development was compiled or kernel-checked for this survey.
We also briefly describe a subsequent application of the signed restriction lemma from family~112 to circuit lower bounds at every fixed depth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

OpenAI's release brought together hundreds of manuscripts addressing longstanding mathematical problems: the Unique Games Conjecture, the exponent of matrix multiplication, Hadwiger's conjecture, the chromatic number of the plane, Kaplansky's conjectures, the amenability of Thompson's group~$F$, the isomorphism problem for free group factors, and many others. Several are accompanied by Lean formalizations; most are not. None had been peer-reviewed at the time of writing.

The motivation for this project is to treat the release as a source of mathematical ideas. A proof is more than a certificate that its theorem is true: it is a store of methods. When a long proof of a famous conjecture is correct, the devices that make it work can have value beyond the theorem; even when an argument fails, a lemma or construction within it may remain valid. The purpose of this project is to mine the release for those devices. We call them \emph{instruments}: new objects, structures, inequalities or proof architectures that a proof introduces in order to reach its conclusion, together with new settings in which established tools are applied.

The project has two goals.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Extracting new instruments.} To isolate and describe the new lemmas, constructions and proof mechanisms generated in these arguments. Once their statements, proofs and relation to earlier work are understood, they can contribute to the shared mathematical knowledge base.
\item \textbf{Making the source arguments accessible.} To give readers a consistent account of what each selected paper claims, which instruments it introduces, which established results it uses, and which steps require closer examination.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A case study in reuse.} After extraction, we investigate whether particular instruments can be reused. A separate application manuscript studies the signed restriction lemma identified in family~\hyperref[fam:112]{112}. The lemma controls correlations through signed combinations of narrow CNFs with bounded expected coefficient mass. Combining it with adaptive averaging, classical depth reduction and quantitative sparsification yields, for the same polynomial-time language as in the source, the gate-count lower bound
\[
 S_D(f_n)\ge 2^{\Omega_D\left((n\log n/\log\log n)^{1/(D-1)}\right)}
 \qquad\text{for every fixed }D\ge3
\]
at all sufficiently large input lengths~$n$. Here $S_D$ counts AND/OR gates in exact circuits of depth at most~$D$, with arbitrary finite fan-in and fan-out and input negations. The source supplies the lemma and the language; the application extends the source's stated depth-three result. The proof will be presented in the separate application paper. This case illustrates the progression from extracting an instrument to investigating its use and obtaining a further theorem.

\paragraph{What this paper contains.} We report an extraction and preliminary novelty assessment for 38 families, selected for the prominence of their problems. For each family, the initial extraction examined the source argument to identify candidate new instruments and distinguish them from combinations of known tools. The paper contains:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{map} of the 38 families (Tables~\ref{tab:map1} and~\ref{tab:map2}), giving for each the result, its Lean status, its central extracted instrument and a provisional novelty assessment;
\item \emph{eleven cross-cutting patterns} (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}) describing recurring approaches in the source proofs, from ``existence instead of construction'' to ``established methods in new settings'' and ``rediscovery without citation'';
\item \emph{family-by-family cards} (Sections~\ref{sec:cards} and~\ref{sec:cards2}) that state each claim, describe the selected candidate instruments with section and lemma references, list what is reused, and record caveats;
\item a \emph{verification and dependency scoreboard} (Section~\ref{sec:scoreboard}) recording formalization status, statement faithfulness, dependence on other OpenAI manuscripts, and uncited nearest ancestors;
\item an explicit account of the \emph{limits of the extraction and mathematical review} (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}



\paragraph{Status of the results.} Throughout, ``the paper proves'' and similar phrases describe what a manuscript \emph{claims} and how its argument is organized. The manuscripts have not been peer-reviewed. Formalization statuses are those recorded during the initial extraction as of 11 October 2026, with the source-status corrections noted below. No Lean project was compiled during either stage. Verdicts of novelty mean ``new relative to the citable literature examined during the initial extraction'', not established priority.

\section{Background and motivation}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The challenge of reading a large release}
A release of this size poses several practical difficulties for readers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Volume.} Some manuscripts run to 170--200 pages, and several families contain closely related papers. Reading even a selected part of the release requires substantial time.
\item \textbf{Technical density.} Many arguments involve nested limits, numerous parameters, and compactness or ultrafilter constructions. Identifying the central mechanism can require tracing dependencies across many sections.
\item \textbf{No peer review.} The manuscripts have not passed through the ordinary refereeing process, and the repository's own formalization catalogue marks review status ``unchecked''.
\item \textbf{Need for accessible descriptions.} A short account of an instrument can help readers decide where to begin in a long source argument, while leaving the correctness of that argument to further examination.
\end{itemize}
These difficulties make structured descriptions useful: they provide starting points for reading, comparison and verification.

\subsection{Extracting the instruments within the proofs}
The project begins with the mathematical content inside each selected argument. We identify the objects, lemmas and proof mechanisms it introduces, trace their roles in the proof, and compare them with earlier work. The family entries present the results of that extraction in a common format. Whether a particular instrument can be used elsewhere is a subsequent question, illustrated by the application described in the introduction.

\subsection{Why this matters}
We see several ways in which a structured extraction of this kind can be useful beyond the immediate question of which claims are correct. Some benefits concern access to the source arguments; others, including further mathematical applications, depend on the validity of the instruments concerned.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \textbf{A catalogue of extracted instruments.} Instruments are described in a form detached from their headline theorems: for example, a ``shared-leg entropy inequality'' for points of Strassen's asymptotic spectrum (\hyperref[fam:107]{107}), a ``latent alphabet gadget'' with noise that rarely changes the honest decoder's output (\hyperref[fam:102]{102}), ``triangular polynomial cells'' with descending precision budgets (\hyperref[fam:159]{159}), or a ``coupled purity bootstrap'' as a spectral-gap criterion (\hyperref[fam:268]{268}). Their statements and proofs can be studied separately from the headline claims before possible applications are investigated.
\item \textbf{Cross-field transfer of mechanisms.} One recurring pattern is ``established methods in new settings'' (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}): a published theorem applied in a setting for which the initial comparison found no precedent, such as the Benyamini--Sternfeld fixed-point-free Lipschitz map against amenability, Braverman's theorem on bounded-depth circuits for Chowla, or Quot-scheme localization for Bloch's conjecture. Describing these transfers gives readers concrete examples to investigate in other settings.
\item \textbf{Directing verification effort.} The cards and scoreboard identify steps on which the conclusions depend, dependencies on other unverified manuscripts, and gaps in the formalization coverage inspected. These details help a reader choose which statements or arguments to check first.
\item \textbf{Reducing the cost of initial reading.} A short entry introduces a manuscript that may extend to two hundred pages. It points to the proposed new instruments and distinguishes them from established ingredients, giving a specialist a route into the full proof.
\item \textbf{A method for examining further releases.} The same extraction method can be applied to other large collections. A fixed protocol (one AI reading per family, usually up to three candidate devices traced through the proof, a literature search per device, a four-grade verdict, and an explicit list of caveats and dependencies) is a template that the community can apply, extend, and audit in turn.
\item \textbf{Scaffolding for formalization.} By isolating the exact statement, the load-bearing lemmas, and the external and internal dependencies of each result, the extraction indicates where formalization effort should be directed, and which existing formal developments still require independent compilation and kernel re-checking.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What the extraction does not do}
The extraction is not a verification of the source proofs; selected statements and identities were checked. Its novelty assessments are limited by the literature examined, and fresh searches were not completed for every family in the second group. The selection covers 38 of 372 families and favors prominent problems. The instruments identified here are devices within proofs; the extraction did not identify a new foundational theory comparable in scope to motives or perfectoid spaces. Section~\ref{sec:limits} gives the full limits of the extraction and mathematical review.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Method, and what ``new'' means here}{Method, and what new means here}}\label{sec:method}

The work proceeded in two stages. The \emph{initial extraction} identified candidate mathematical instruments, compared them with the literature, and assigned preliminary novelty assessments. The \emph{subsequent mathematical review} checked the reported theorem statements and selected supporting arguments and revised the draft. These terms distinguish the two activities throughout the paper. Both groups of families---the first 17 and the second 21---belong to the initial extraction.

During the initial extraction, a separate AI reading was carried out for each family, using the LaTeX source where the repository provides it (most do, under \texttt{build/\allowbreak{}sections/\allowbreak{}}), otherwise the PDF, together with the Lean challenge and solution files and, where released, the abridged reasoning trace. Each reading generally isolated up to three candidate devices, traced each through the proof, then searched the literature for the device and its nearest relatives. Verdicts use four grades.

\begin{description}
\item[New] an object, structure, inequality or proof architecture with no precedent found, even though its parts are classical.
\item[New combination] the ingredients are established, while their combination or application appears new.
\item[Incremental] a refinement or variation of an established technique.
\item[Unclear] the available text and comparisons did not support a definite classification.
\end{description}

The subsequent mathematical review compared theorem statements and selected supporting arguments against a pinned repository snapshot and corrected mathematical statements, hypotheses and counts. Potential extensions, including the proposed logarithm analogue of family 017, are distinguished from consequences actually established here. Literature comparisons and novelty assessments originate in the initial extraction; the subsequent mathematical review did not repeat every search.

Literature-search coverage varied across the two groups. For some entries in the second group, comparison relied on directly retrieved primary sources and the model's existing knowledge without a fresh search. The affected entries identify this limitation. These comparisons are not an exhaustive prior-art search.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Cross-cutting patterns}{Cross-cutting patterns}}\label{sec:patterns}

Several approaches recur across the selected proofs. The following patterns describe how the arguments establish existence, control parameters, transfer results between settings, and introduce auxiliary structures. They provide a way to compare instruments arising in different subjects.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Existence instead of construction}{Existence instead of construction}}

Several arguments establish existence through compactness, spectral duality, random sampling, or a terminating search whose output has not been computed. The resulting existence statement need not supply an object of practical size.

The proof of $\omega \le 9/4$ bounds the asymptotic spectrum without providing a competitive finite matrix size. The Hadwiger argument selects m through an ultrafilter, and the Hilbert 10 reduction uses a nonstandard root. The zero-divisor and direct-finiteness constructions use random choices or searches with parameters such as $q=2^{217}$ and $n\gtrsim e^{300000}$. In the second group, the Hilbert 16 argument gives an ineffective B(d), the nearby-Lagrangian example has no explicit dimension, and the Borsuk construction supplies a continuous set without extracting a finite subset. These examples differ in effectivity, but each leaves some construction data unspecified or unevaluated.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Estimates become extremal problems}{Estimates become extremal problems}}

A proposed inequality can be studied through the regularity of its optimal-exponent function. Assuming that the desired bound fails, differentiation of this function imposes conditions on an extremal configuration. The proof then rules out a configuration satisfying those conditions.

In the three-dimensional Kakeya argument, coordinatewise monotonicity gives local BV regularity and approximate differentiability almost everywhere. An additional monotonicity comparison yields ordinary total differentiability, and the partial derivatives determine the relevant plank shapes. The four-dimensional argument uses a ``horizon'' defined by an infimum over quadratic fits. In the Erd\H{o}s argument, a potential controls the loss at each weight using only the higher weights.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Entropy and information as proof devices}{Entropy and information as proof devices}}

Mutual information, relative-entropy projections, and entropy inequalities organize estimates in several proofs. The information quantities measure how much dependence or conditioning is available, and incompatible upper and lower bounds produce a contradiction.

The Kakeya proof uses a mutual-information bound to apply a projection theorem to dependent events. The Hadwiger construction combines Kleitman--Winston recording with a Csiszár I-projection as its progress measure and a Ford--Fulkerson cut as its stopping certificate. The matrix-multiplication argument for $\omega$ uses an entropy inequality for a shared tensor leg, while the zero-divisor construction bounds repeated traversals of a random graph by entropy. In the second group, conditioning in the ionization argument costs $\log^5(e/p)$ times the inverse squared noise scale in energy. The area-law proof expresses conditional mutual information through a log-norm derivative, and the Haldane-gap proof uses a Rényi-2 purity bootstrap.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Parity and characters over finite fields}{Parity and characters over finite fields}}

Four constructions use cancellation in finite fields, either through a scalar identity or through vanishing character sums.

The direct-finiteness construction requires $\ell+1=0$ in K. The zero-divisor construction uses projective lines whose pairwise intersections have odd size. The UGC gadget is defined over $\F_2^s$ and contains a quadratic block over $\F_{2^d}$. The proof of $\omega \le 9/4$ applies a DFT across $L=5M$ copies to select exactly the matching labels.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Rigidity theorems that erase regularity hypotheses}{Rigidity theorems that erase regularity hypotheses}}

Some arguments replace a regularity assumption with a transfer theorem. The theorem connects the unrestricted object to a measurable, constructible, or otherwise controlled formulation, so that the remaining proof can use that formulation.

For plane coloring, the transfer states that a proper coloring exists exactly when a weak measurable coloring exists. The Hilbert--Smith argument uses sheaf categories generated by arbitrary continuous maps from polyhedra while retaining invertible Verdier duality. The Hilbert 10 argument uses an oracle reduction without constructing a definable copy of $\mathbb{Z}$.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Established methods in new settings}{Established methods in new settings}}

A recurring contribution is the application of an established theorem in a different mathematical setting. The relevant novelty question concerns that application and its surrounding argument; the imported theorem retains its original attribution.

The examples include the Benyamini--Sternfeld theorem of 1983 on Lipschitz maps without approximate fixed points in an amenability argument; Gross\textquoteright{}s 2019 conformal Skorokhod map in the Mahler proof; and Kubota--Patterson cubic theta theory from 1977 in a zero-free-region argument. Other transfers use Fox calculus and free Poisson laws for free group factors, Lelong numbers for volume products, and Lipton--Tarjan separators for diagrams of van Kampen type. The second group applies Braverman\textquoteright{}s 2009 $\mathrm{AC}^{0}$ theorem to Chowla correlations, Marian--Oprea--Pandharipande localization to Bloch\textquoteright{}s conjecture, Lion--Rolin preparation to Hilbert\textquoteright{}s 16th problem, Ueda\textquoteright{}s reduced HNN extensions to Naimark\textquoteright{}s problem, Suzuki\textquoteright{}s quantum transfer matrix to the Haldane gap, and Kubo--Ando means to the area law.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Rediscovery without citation}{Rediscovery without citation}}

The initial comparison identified several constructions closely related to earlier work that was not cited in the manuscript. In some cases, the corresponding reasoning summary does name the earlier source. These comparisons matter when distinguishing a new instrument from a reconstruction or adaptation.

The Vlasov--Maxwell manuscript uses a time-integrated momentum increment related to Cheverry--Ibrahim's work, which is cited six times in the reasoning summary but absent from the paper. The interpolation argument for $\pi$ has antecedents in Masser and Fischler--Nakamaye; the spectral argument for $\omega$ follows a template in Alman--Li's 2026 ``Strassen calculus''; and the Kakeya comparison identifies Rios--Sawyer as an uncited antecedent. In the second group, the M\'ezard--Parisi reasoning summary names Mourrat--Panchenko and Barbier--Panchenko, while the paper omits them. The Serre-positivity argument uses an orbit lemma of Suslin--Voevodsky without citation.

\subsection{Consequences and proposed extensions}

Some entries describe consequences of the stated results or suggest extensions beyond them. Each requires its own hypotheses and argument. The cards distinguish consequences presented as following from the source from adaptations that still need a proof.

The centers $(r^j,jp_i/q_i)$ suggest a possible analogue of the $\pi$ argument for $r\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\setminus\{1\}$. Establishing $\mu(\log r)=2$ would require a separate interpolation, height and determinant argument; it does not follow verbatim from the theorem surveyed here. The claimed free-group-factor isomorphism would refute the Connes--Shlyakhtenko $L^2$-Betti conjecture for $L(\F_n)$, a consequence not discussed in the paper. In the second group, the hyperinvariant-subspace counterexample would also refute Kadison's transitive-algebra problem, which the catalogue summary omits; the Mézard--Parisi lower bound is stated for bounded interactions with permutation-invariant law, odd arity included.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Probability imported into deterministic problems}{Probability imported into deterministic problems}}

Several proofs introduce a random or ergodic auxiliary system into a deterministic problem. Almost-sure statements or measure-zero obstructions in that system then support the deterministic conclusion.

In the hyperinvariant-subspace construction, operators are assembled over the 2-adic odometer, where a finite integer-valued measurable label cannot strictly decrease almost everywhere along the measure-preserving dynamics. The ionization argument smooths and conditions electron positions. Marked Poisson perturbations provide the hierarchy in the Mézard--Parisi proof, while the Baum--Connes construction uses a local-lemma probability law along an infinite tower. The plane-coloring argument uses Haar rigidity for the unrestricted laws considered there.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Auxiliary structure manufactured by the proof}{Auxiliary structure manufactured by the proof}}

An argument may introduce auxiliary structure with properties it can control, and then prove that the original object is constrained by that structure.

The UGC construction introduces a latent alphabet hidden from the honest player. The proof of $\mathrm{L}=\mathrm{BPL}$ uses one shared O(log n)-bit environment for all samplers. In the Mézard--Parisi argument, a finite auxiliary hierarchy is refined only after $N\to\infty$. The ionization proof uses a decreasing chain of observation $\sigma$-fields. The BSD argument introduces tame Iwasawa variables and specializes them to zero at the end.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Label versus content}{Label versus content}}

A title may suppress restrictions that are essential to the mathematical statement. The extraction therefore records the precise model, hypotheses, and conclusion, including computational assumptions where they occur.

``Universal computation in Navier--Stokes'' uses volume-preserving flows and a force defined by the residual; the prescribed forcing properties, including eventual stationarity, remain mathematical constraints to verify. ``Baum--Connes counterexample'' is reduced, coefficient-free rational injectivity for a finitely generated group; finite presentation is not determined, and the claim does not refute the classical Novikov conjecture. ``Three MUBs in dimension six'' rests on a binary64 computation conditional on compiler semantics. ``Ionization conjecture'' is the strict-binding formulation, with threshold bound states not excluded. ``Beyond the square-root exponent'' is $2^{\omega(\sqrt{n})}$ at inverse-tower rate, not $2^{n^{1/2+\varepsilon}}$ for a fixed $\varepsilon>0$.

\item \textbf{Depth shift with charging fresh splits} (\S{}8, Lemma 4.5, Thm 8.2). To extract conditional covariances one divides by coefficients of order $\delta^{2b}$; naively the error sum is O(1). New, as far as could be determined; the trace marks it as the turning point.
\item \textbf{Recursive law encoding of a single site} (Lemma 9.1): conditional magnetizations of a uniform site encode an admissible Panchenko--Talagrand trial law reproducing all prescribed-tree moments. Incremental, a finite-N form of Austin--Panchenko.

\paragraph{Extracted instruments.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Selective bridge via reduced HNN adjunction along peaking sequences} (Thm 4.1). \subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{325 \textperiodcentered{} The complete Crouzeix conjecture}{325 · The complete Crouzeix conjecture}}\label{fam:325}
\badges{Lean, all sizes, matrix-valued, arbitrary Hilbert space \badgesep{} combination leaning new}

For every bounded operator $A$ on a nonzero complex Hilbert space, including infinite-dimensional spaces, and every matrix polynomial $P(z)=\sum_k B_kz^k$, the stated complete inequality is
\[
 \left\|\sum_k A^k\otimes B_k\right\|
 \le2\sup_{z\in W(A)}\|P(z)\|
 =2\max_{z\in\overline{W(A)}}\|P(z)\|.
\]
The constant 2 is sharp. The numerical range need not be closed in infinite dimension, which is why the supremum or its closure is used. The holomorphic extension concerns matrix-valued functions holomorphic on an open neighborhood of $\overline{W(A)}$; rational functions must have all their poles outside $\overline{W(A)}$. In this sense the closure is a complete 2-spectral set. Scalar proofs are presented in the 2026 preprints of Jin, Lorist--Schwenninger and Luo~\cite{src:jin,src:lorist,src:luo}. Lorist--Schwenninger explicitly explain why their proof and Jin's do not directly give the completely bounded version.

\paragraph{Extracted instruments.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ordered density comparison} (direct paper Thm 3.2, main Lemma 5.1). For a finite matrix $A$ and a matrix polynomial $F$ normalized to have boundary supremum norm one on an enclosing analytic convex domain, choose a unit top singular pair $F[A]x=\gamma y$ (the zero case is immediate). Compress the Crouzeix--Palencia positive field $R+R^*\succeq0$ by $[X\ Y]$ into a $2\times2$ block $\Delta$($\lambda$) of $m\times m$ boundary functions whose entries have Faber coefficients $P_k=X^*b_k(A)X$, $M_k=Y^*b_k(A)X$. For PSD $\Delta$, $\mathrm{tr}(\Delta J\Delta J)\ge0$ gives $2|M+L^*|^2_{HS}\le|P+P^*|^2_{HS}+|Q+Q^*|^2_{HS}$; integrate over the circle and apply Parseval. The cross term is at least $a^2=\sum|M_k|^2$; the diagonals are bounded by testing the functional on the two ordered products FG and GF (so no commutation is needed), with the Faber weight 2 giving $\le4a^2/\gamma^2$. Hence $2a^2\le8a^2/\gamma^2$ and $\gamma \le 2$ when $a>0$. If $a=0$, the cross functional vanishes, so its value on $F$ gives $\gamma=0$. The bounded-operator conclusion is obtained through finite compressions and approximation, without assuming a norm-attaining vector in infinite dimension. This replaces Crouzeix--Palencia's operator-norm triangle inequality ($1+\sqrt{2}$) and Lorist--Schwenninger's perturbation lemma, whose commutation hypothesis ``is not available after matrix amplification''. Combination leaning new: extremal pairs and the positive double layer are known; measuring the compressed block in boundary $L^{2}$(Hilbert--Schmidt) had no precedent in the literature examined.
\item \textbf{Exterior Faber coefficients and the positive kernel}: the Kövari--Pommerenke and Beckermann weight-2 bound transplanted from $H^\infty$ to matrix $L^{2}$. Incremental.
\item \textbf{Optimal metric and endpoint extremal} (main Thm 1.2): for a finite matrix and a bounded convex domain with regular real-analytic boundary strictly enclosing its numerical range, the Stein LMI attains the squared minimal condition number, with $\kappa\in[1,2]$; SDP duality plus a unitary-colligation realization gives an exact extremal; a common positive boundary representation follows from Sz.-Nagy. Combination of textbook interpolation theory.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reused.}

Delyon--Delyon and Crouzeix--Palencia double-layer positivity; exterior conformal maps and Faber polynomials; Paulsen's similarity theorem; Stein 1952; Sz.-Nagy dilation; Katsnelson--Kheifets--Yuditskii colligations.

\paragraph{Caveats.}

The structural theorem applies separately to each enclosing domain strictly containing $\overline{W(A)}$; no limiting similarity on the closure is asserted. Luo's scalar argument also uses a top singular pair~\cite{src:luo}, so that ingredient alone is not evidence of novelty. The relation between its boundary certificates and the matrix-valued argument here requires closer comparison.

Comparator challenge files contain target statements, often with \texttt{sorry} placeholders. Their JSON configurations point to separate solution modules; a placeholder in a challenge does not determine whether its solution is complete. For example, \texttt{GeneralMahler.json} points to a solution theorem distinct from its challenge, and the 003 configurations point to zeta, Dirichlet and Hecke declarations. These files were read, not compiled, and their transitive dependencies were not exhaustively inspected. Textual inspection and standard-axiom configurations do not establish kernel verification of the 19 labeled families. The configurations inspected in the subsequent mathematical review disable the optional nanoda recheck.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Limits of the extraction and mathematical review}{Limits of the extraction and mathematical review}}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reading is not verification.} The initial extraction and subsequent mathematical review included checks of selected statements, hypotheses and identities; those checks do not independently certify every underlying proof. No Lean formalization was built or kernel-checked during either stage. Absence of a check in this survey says nothing about whether other people have checked a manuscript.
\item \textbf{Novelty assessments depend on the search scope.} ``No precedent found'' means that the literature comparison described in the card found no matching instrument; a device in a 1980s Russian paper or an unpublished talk could be missed. The verdicts are provisional judgments relative to the literature searched; the subsequent mathematical review did not independently validate them, and mixed labels are not counted as disjoint classes.
\item \textbf{Choice of thirty-eight.} The release has 372 families; this set was selected for the prominence of the problems, which favors major conjectures and particularly strong claims. Families centered on improvements to existing bounds were not examined systematically.
\item \textbf{Search coverage.} Some entries in the second group used directly retrieved primary sources without a fresh literature search. Their novelty assessments have correspondingly narrower support.
\item \textbf{Priority and AI lineage.} At least four results have close human or AI-assisted neighbours predating the release (Naimark: Tanaka; Crouzeix: Jin, Lorist--Schwenninger, Luo; Borsuk: a 63-dimensional GitHub note; Serre positivity: a December 2025 AI note). This survey records these; it does not adjudicate priority.
\item \textbf{Scope of the extracted novelty.} The extraction identified devices within proofs. It did not identify a new foundational theory, a new category of object comparable to motives or perfectoid spaces, or a new conjecture. This describes what the extraction found in the selected families; it is not an exhaustive claim about their contents or the full release.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and future directions}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper extracts candidate new mathematical instruments from 38 families in OpenAI's October 2026 release. The family entries describe their roles, antecedents and caveats; the maps, eleven cross-cutting patterns and dependency scoreboard make those descriptions easier to compare. The initial assessments identify new constructions and proof mechanisms, applications of established theorems in different settings, and adaptations whose earlier sources need closer acknowledgment. These findings remain subject to the limits of the literature comparisons and mathematical checks reported here.

The project begins with extraction and then asks what can be established and used. The signed-restriction case study illustrates that sequence: an instrument identified in a source paper becomes an input to a further theorem. This is the reason to investigate the release as a treasure mine. Three directions would extend the work:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \textbf{Independent verification.} The cards identify where human checking would matter most: headline proofs not independently checked here (among them Hilbert's tenth problem over~$\mathbb{Q}$, the rational Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties, Kakeya, Erd\H{o}s's reciprocal-sum conjecture, Hilbert--Smith and the general Mahler conjecture), load-bearing dependencies on other unverified manuscripts (for example family~\hyperref[fam:004]{004} on family~\hyperref[fam:010]{010}), and specific flagged steps such as Proposition~5.14 of family~\hyperref[fam:002]{002} or the proposed extension of family~\hyperref[fam:017]{017} to $\mu(\log r)=2$ for $r\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\setminus\{1\}$, which requires a separate argument. Expert refereeing of these points, and of the novelty verdicts based on incomplete literature searches, would strengthen these assessments.
\item \textbf{Formalization.} The nineteen original headline labels include partial inspection for 003 and 087 and are not a count of fully verified results. Repository documentation and linked solution modules should first be reconciled theorem by theorem, including the additional coverage reported for 007 and 159, then compiled and kernel-checked independently. Companion proofs must be kept separate from their families' headline statements.
\item \textbf{Extending the extraction to all 372 families.} The extraction covers a sample selected for prominent problems. Applying the same protocol to the whole release, including families that improve existing bounds, would turn the catalogue of instruments into a comprehensive, searchable resource, indexed by mechanism as well as by problem, and would test whether the patterns found here hold across the release.
\end{enumerate}
